Briefing

Your task

You are advising Rahul Mehta, VP for R&D at Sigma Tech Limited. Project S is a Rs 500 million sensor-fusion programme: Rs 300 million now, and a further Rs 200 million a year from now — but only if the first year’s results warrant it. Across four stages you will (1) value Project S the way Iyer did, as one committed outlay, (2) build the decision tree yourself and value it as a staged investment, then step into a walk-away lab where you make the continue-or-walk call at each node and put a price on walking away, (3) discover that the gap between those two answers is a real option, worth naming and pricing properly, and (4) confront the governance question that decides whether any of it is real.

No option-pricing formulas in Stages 1 & 2. Everything through the decision tree is expected-value arithmetic and present value at Sigma Tech’s twenty per cent hurdle rate — exactly what the teaching note calls it: “the entire quantitative burden is a two-period decision tree solved by backward induction.” Option theory arrives afterwards, as a way of seeing what you have already built.

You are scored on four dimensions — Accuracy (your arithmetic against the verified figures), Judgment (the continue/stop calls and concept questions), Options (mapping the case onto option theory, and pricing the flexibility), and Governance (what would make the option real).

Stage 2½ · The walk-away lab

Make the t = 1 call yourself — continue or walk away?

It is June 2027. Mehta now knows whether STL is in Scenario O or Scenario P. At each node he can continue — pay the Rs 200 million second tranche for a year-two asset worth its continuation value — or walk away. Toggle each node and watch the staged NPV and the value of the flexibility move. Iyer’s committed basis is simply “continue at both nodes, whatever happens”.

Walking away is rarely free

Stopping has a price of its own: severance for a team already hired, cancellation charges on tooling orders, a customer who was told to expect a product. Drag the slider to put a number on the cost of walking away. The node cards and readouts above update with it, and the chart traces what the flexibility is worth at every exit cost.

Look for two points on the curve: the exit cost at which the staged NPV falls to zero, and the exit cost beyond which the flexibility is worth nothing at all. Then register your reading below.

Stage 3 · Real options

The same tree, a different lens

Look again at what you just built. At t = 1, STL has the right, not the obligation, to pay Rs 200 million for whatever the year-two cash flow turns out to be worth. That is a call option. The Rs 300 million you already spent buys two things at once: a certain year-one cash flow, and this option on year two.

The payoff, drawn

The option pays STL the amount by which the year-two asset is worth more than the Rs 200 million it costs to acquire — and nothing when it is worth less. Confirm the two payoffs from your own Stage 2 numbers.

Value it as an avoided loss

The teaching note is blunt about this: seeing the option value as a leftover from subtracting two NPVs means you have not understood it yet. Seeing it as the value of a loss STL avoids means you have. Work it from the P branch.

Advanced & optional — price the option properly, with risk-neutral probabilities

The teaching note flags this as a natural extension, not a requirement. Everything above discounted the option’s payoff at the same 20% rate used for the underlying operating cash flows. Formal option pricing does not do that — it builds a risk-neutral probability from the up and down movements of the underlying, and discounts at the risk-free rate instead. Stage 4 · Krishnan’s challenge (Question 4)

Is the second tranche genuinely discretionary?

Anjali Desai: “We are not being asked to spend five hundred million rupees today. We are being asked to spend three hundred. The other two hundred is a decision we make next June, when we will know something we do not know now.”
Krishnan: “Is that actually true? Or is it discretionary in the model and mandatory in the room, because by next June we will have hired the team and told the customer, and nobody will want to be the person who stopped it?”

Nobody in the room answered him. The staged valuation is worth Rs 22.22 million only if STL will actually stop at the P node — and, as the walk-away lab showed, only if stopping is cheap. Select every safeguard below that would make the option real rather than an accounting convenience.
