ROOM 11 · MONDAY TO SATURDAY · 9:40 TO 10:35
Dr. Savita Deshmukh has taught this course for twenty-two years

Sixty-five students. One notebook of lecture notes so refined that juniors borrow it before she has finished writing it. Her unit-test averages have sat within two marks of the same number for a decade.

22 years 65 students averages flat, ten years
The feedback forms say it, "sometimes twice in the same batch": "Madam's notes are perfect."
THE FEEDBACK FORM
"We remember everything till the exam"

Read the sentence twice. The first half is praise. The second half is a timestamp — the remembering has a shelf life, and the exam is its expiry date.

Nobody wrote "we can apply this". Nobody wrote "we argued about it". The form was never asked — but what the students chose to praise is the class they received.

AN OPEN QUESTION · NO ANSWERS INSIDE

If the notes are perfect and the scores are flat for ten years, what exactly is Room 11 producing?

DOWN THE CORRIDOR · THREE YEARS INTO THE JOB
Prateek lets them argue

Three years into the job, boards always half-erased, syllabus always half-behind. He calls it "letting them argue": one real situation, two teams defending opposite decisions. Some feedback: "his class is noisy". Other feedback: "the only class I don't bunk".

His test averages are no better than Savita's. But at the department seminar, his students are the only ones asking questions.

AN OPEN QUESTION

Same syllabus, same averages. What is different in the room — and is it teaching?

THE SAME WEEK · SIX WEEKS BEFORE THE NAAC REPORT IS DUE
Two things happen in one week
THE CIRCULAR

The principal asks every department to "document learner-centric pedagogical practices and their measurable outcomes" for the NAAC self-study report.

THE HOD

"Your notes are famous, Savita tai. But the committee will ask what the student does in your class. Show me something for the report."

SAVITA

Twenty-two years of practice — and, for the first time, a request for evidence instead of admiration.

AN OPEN QUESTION

What does the circular actually want — new methods, or proof of outcomes? Would it be satisfied by the same syllabus and the same test, if something else changed?

THAT NIGHT — TUESDAY’S PLAN, AND THE DECISION SHE CANNOT DUCK
Unit 4, delivered twenty-one times

Tuesday's plan: Unit 4, a chapter she had delivered twenty-one times, fifty-five minutes, comfortably ahead of the syllabus calendar. She could teach it in her sleep. The students, she suspects, could hear it in hers.

Deliver it for the twenty-second time — or redesign one class, one chapter, and find out what her room can produce besides notes. What does the committee want to see? What does she want to see?

THE DILEMMA, STATED

If she redesigns only Tuesday — one class, one chapter — what single change buys the most learning per minute, and what will it cost her in coverage?

NOW THE CASE IS YOURS
Seven questions (one optional). Zero answers.
  1. 1

    What is Savita's class actually producing, term after term? Name it precisely — is it learning?

  2. 2

    Prateek's test averages are no better than Savita's. Is he a better teacher or a more popular one? What evidence would decide it?

  3. 3

    What does the circular want — new methods, or evidence of outcomes? Would the same syllabus and the same test satisfy it if something else changed?

  4. 4

    If Savita redesigns only Tuesday, what single change buys the most learning per minute — and what does it cost in coverage?

  5. 5

    What should she NOT change?

  6. 6

    You are her head of department. Sketch the fifty-five minutes — and state what you would need to see by Friday to know it worked.

  7. 7

    Optional: the committee asks for "measurable outcomes". What would you measure — and what had her decade of flat scores already been measuring?
