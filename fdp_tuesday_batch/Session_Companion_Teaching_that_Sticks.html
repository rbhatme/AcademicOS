Teaching that sticks

This session is taught by the method it teaches: a case read cold, questions asked before answers, concepts arriving only after you have committed your own words, and a quiz that measures nothing but your next class.

Use ← → to move. D switches the light, F fills the screen, R (twice) resets the quiz and the exercises. The questions on each page are for arguing over — they are deliberately left unanswered.

Case: The Tuesday Batch — twenty-two years of perfect notes, prepared by Ramesh Bhat for faculty discussion (November 2026). All characters and institutions are fictional. NAAC references are general; check the current NAAC framework before you cite it.

1 · THE PROBLEM (SCENE)
Room 11, 9:40, Unit 4 on the plan

Dr. Savita Deshmukh has taught the course for twenty-two years. Monday to Saturday, 9:40 to 10:35, Room 11: 65 students, one notebook of lecture notes so refined juniors borrow it before she has finished writing it. Unit-test averages: within two marks of the same number for a decade. The feedback forms say "Madam's notes are perfect," sometimes twice in one batch. "We remember everything till the exam."

Down the corridor, Prateek — three years in, boards always half-erased, syllabus always half-behind — has started "letting them argue": one real situation, two teams defending opposite decisions. "His class is noisy," say some forms; "the only class I don't bunk," say others. Same test averages. But his students are the only ones asking questions at the department seminar.

4 · BEHIND DOOR B — CASE METHOD
Case method: judgment under ambiguity

A decision-forcing story: real stakes, incomplete information, a protagonist whose shoes the student must occupy — and a commitment extracted before the reveal. Fits Apply, Analyse and Evaluate; it is the method this very session is running on you.

PROS

Retention follows discovery; discussion and defence skills; a safe rehearsal of real judgment; the class remembers the argument, not the slide.

CONS

Heavy faculty prep and facilitation; quality hinges on case selection; costs coverage; needs cold-read discipline and a strict clock.

SAVITA FIT · 65 SEATS

One short case a week is feasible in Room 11 — this session is the proof of concept. Start with a ten-minute vignette, cold-read on paper, two questions, one commit.

4 · BEHIND DOOR B — ROLE PLAY
Role play: the other side of the table

Students take assigned positions — the client, the auditor, the regulator, the sceptical buyer — and argue in role. It fits ethics, negotiation and anything with two defensible answers; Bloom Understand through Evaluate.

PROS

Almost free to run; high energy; builds empathy and surfaces hidden assumptions; the argument is remembered because it was your own.

CONS

Uneven participation and stage fright; can slide into theatre without stakes; assessment is subjective — needs a rubric for observers as well as players.

SAVITA FIT · 65 SEATS

Works if every single student has a job: eight in role, the rest as observers with a checklist that feeds the debrief. Nobody spectates.

4 · BEHIND DOOR B — SIMULATION
Simulation: a semester of consequences in an hour

A model of a system with decision rounds and feedback — markets, runs, negotiations, cash budgets. Students decide, the system answers, the debrief names what happened. Fits systems thinking; Bloom Apply and Analyse.

PROS

Compresses time; failure is safe and reversible; feedback is immediate; the affect makes the lesson durable — the figure is remembered because it hurt.

CONS

Fidelity gap: the model's simplifications can teach themselves; setup and logistics are the heaviest on this menu; students may play the mechanics, not the concept.

SAVITA FIT · 65 SEATS

The most setup-heavy door — borrow before you build. Ready-made simulations exist for most subjects; the debrief is where Savita earns her keep.

4 · BEHIND DOOR B — PROJECTS
Projects: the artifact is the evidence

An authentic deliverable with milestones — a research report, a model, a client memo. The assessment is the artifact itself. This is the only method that lives at Create, and the one NAAC reviewers can hold in their hands.

PROS

Authentic integration of skills; ownership drives motivation; produces portfolio evidence — the "measurable outcomes" the circular asks for, in physical form.

CONS

Slow feedback loop; team free-riding; demands rubrics, milestones and ruthless scope control; hard to fit more than one or two in a term.

SAVITA FIT · 65 SEATS

One mini-project per semester beats none — start formative, mark it against a rubric, and let the best three become teaching exhibits for the next batch.

4 · BEHIND DOOR B — FLIPPED CLASSROOM
Flipped classroom: first exposure at home, verbs in class

The reading or the video carries the first exposure; class time is spent on the higher verbs with the expert present. It is a resequencing, not a gadget — and for Savita it has a sweet twist.

PROS

Scarce face-to-face minutes go to application; students control the pace of first exposure; misconceptions surface in class where they can be fixed.

CONS

Lives or dies on pre-work compliance; watching a video is not learning; drifts back to lecture unless the class tasks are genuinely higher-order.

SAVITA FIT · 65 SEATS

Her famous notes are the perfect pre-work. She does not retire them — she redeploys them: "the notes become the reading, not the class." Twenty-two years of craft, repurposed.

4 · BEHIND DOOR B — PEER INSTRUCTION
Peer instruction: everyone thinks, not just the front row

One well-designed question → every student commits alone → pairs defend their answers → selected pairs share, the room votes. Ninety seconds to two minutes, no infrastructure, any discipline. Bloom Understand through Analyse.

PROS

Scales to any room with zero cost; makes thinking visible in ninety seconds; the individual commit prevents the confident few from answering for the silent many.

CONS

Shallow without well-designed questions — the question is the method; needs ruthless time-boxing and a tolerance for noise.

SAVITA FIT · 65 SEATS

The lowest-cost insert into her existing fifty-five minutes — this is Door D in practice, and the natural first step for Tuesday.

4 · BEHIND DOOR B — DESIGN THINKING
Design thinking: empathy before answers

A human-centred, iterative cycle — empathise → define → ideate → prototype → test — for problems that have no single right answer to find, only a better one to design. Fits interdisciplinary topics and wicked problems; Bloom Evaluate and Create.

PROS

Anchors solutions in the user's reality; failure becomes cheap, fast and informative — prototype, learn, redesign; builds tolerance for iteration.

CONS

The ambiguity disorients students trained on right-answer assessments; iterations eat time that fixed periods rarely hold.

SAVITA FIT · 65 SEATS

One period can run a micro-cycle: empathise with one real student profile, sketch two interventions, prototype Tuesday's ten minutes, and let a partner test it.

4 · BEHIND DOOR B — STUDIO & CRITIQUE
Studio & critique: the apprenticeship pattern

The atelier model: demonstration, sustained making, public display, and continuous verbal critique — with teacher talk kept scarce and precious. It teaches what words cannot: tacit mastery acquired by watching experts and handling real materials.

PROS

Tacit learning and authentic mastery; the room becomes a haven where failure is a step, not a verdict; builds observation, persistence and reflection.

CONS

Master–apprentice hierarchy can become servitude unless critique runs both ways; demands space and long incubation blocks that timetables rarely allow.

SAVITA FIT · 65 SEATS

It does not need an art room. "Display working drafts, not final answers": pin three partial solutions to the wall of the board, and let the class critique them.

CRITICAL QUESTIONWhat could your students make, display, and have critiqued — instead of submit and forget?

4 · BEHIND DOOR B — DESIRABLE DIFFICULTIES
Desirable difficulties: harder is better

The learning-science wing of the menu: interleave the practice (ABCABC, not AAABBB), space the retrieval, and make students generate before you tell. Difficulty is not the enemy of learning — fluent ease is.

PROS

Durable retention and transfer across contexts; defeats the illusion of competence — Savita's students felt they had learned till the exam; the exam disagreed.

CONS

It feels worse in the short run: students mistake productive struggle for poor teaching, and the teacher must resist optimising for smooth lessons.

SAVITA FIT · 65 SEATS

The cheapest redesign on this menu: reorder her practice questions, space her quizzes, make the class generate before she explains — and tell them WHY it feels harder.

CRITICAL QUESTIONWhere does your course optimise for smoothness — fluent lessons, easy practice — at the cost of durability?

4 · BEHIND DOOR B — AI-ENABLED TEACHING
AI-enabled teaching: orchestrate the friction

Used without pedagogical design, AI invites three risks: cognitive offloadingthe fluency heuristicepistemic narrowing — bypassing effortful thinking, mistaking linguistic polish for factual accuracy, and converging on single-perspective answers. The faculty role in an AI-enabled course: cognitive orchestrator, not a passive observer of technology.

10 · ANALYSIS II — DESIGNING BACKWARD, SCALING UP
Backward design & the 65-seat classroom

Backward design reverses the habit: decide the outcome, then the acceptable evidence, and only then the activities. It prices Door C and Door D honestly — you cannot claim a learner-centric session without saying what evidence it will produce.

And scale is an excuse, not a barrier: think-pair-share runs in a 65-seat hall with structure and a timer; the one-minute paper gives you formative evidence in sixty seconds with zero marking. Beware the famous "retention pyramid" percentages — widely cited, evidence-weak; a room of faculty should model source hygiene.

Know your instruments when the evidence arrives: assessment gathers the data; evaluation interprets it and closes the loop. Direct measures — marks against stated targets — and indirect ones — student, employer and alumni voice — triangulate. Savita's decade never ran the loop: that, more than the lecture, is the failure.

11 · ANALYSIS III — WRITING WHAT EXCELLENCE LOOKS LIKE
The three-part outcome statement
CONDITION

Given what situation, data or case?

PERFORMANCE

What will the student do? An observable action verb.

MEASURABLE CRITERION

How well must they do it?

"Understanding ratios" is a hope; this is a writable outcome: "Given financial statements, students will compute the ratios and identify the company's problems, with 80% of students doing so at a stated standard." Three parts, one sentence — Savita's "show me something" becomes writable the moment it becomes a three-part sentence.

Avoid the traps: "case discussion" is an activity, not an outcome; vague verbs — understand, appreciate, familiarise — cannot be assessed; showing, knowing or describing without a measurement standard is not an outcome. And when the evidence arrives, grade it with a rubric — task, scale, dimensions, and a description of each performance level — so grading is consistent and students see what excellence looks like before submission.

12 · APPLICATION — YOUR OWN CLASSROOM
Redesign ten minutes of your next class

Take the next class you will teach, any subject. Choose ten minutes you currently lecture. Redesign them so the students perform one higher-order verb — apply, analyse or evaluate — on the spot. Then defend it to a partner: what will you be able to SEE that you cannot see today?

Then add one line your students will see: in this ten minutes, AI is prohibited / permitted / encouraged / required for ______, and I will grade ______. If you cannot finish that sentence, you do not yet know what the redesign is for.
