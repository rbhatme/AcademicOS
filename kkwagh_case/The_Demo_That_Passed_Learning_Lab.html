Phase A · The Board’s minute · 1 of 4
A good name in the fields, and it knew it

Nirjhar Agro Systems had a good name in the fields of western Maharashtra and knew it. Founded twenty-seven years earlier by Madhukar Kale, it made drip-irrigation controllers, valves and solar pumps. Farmers said a Nirjhar controller “never breaks”, and its field service was the best in the region. Growth, though, had slowed from eight per cent a year to five, and two well-funded agritech start-ups had begun selling cheap soil sensors with a free advisory app. Nobody at Nirjhar thought the company was in trouble. A few people, privately, thought it had stopped moving.

Aditya Mahajan had been Managing Director for nine months. Before that he had spent sixteen years at Nirjhar, the last six as Head of Operations, so he knew every plant and every member of the leadership team. In his first months he mostly listened. One question he asked everyone was, “What does a farmer actually buy from us?” Most people answered with a list of products. A few said “a better crop, with less water and less worry”. He noticed the difference and kept it to himself.

Nirjhar at a glance
₹420crore in annual sales, with 850 employees
2.1lakh farms running a Nirjhar controller
600dealers in five states
8→5per cent a year: growth slowing as start-ups arrive

Phase B · Thirty farms · 1 of 6
“The app does not know my vines”

Tanvi’s task, from Neha, was to “pull the app numbers for the demo slide”. The numbers were good: 1.2 lakh downloads and 46,000 monthly active users, up thirty per cent in a year. But the same data showed something the dashboard did not. Of the 31,000 farms that had switched on Saathi’s automatic schedule, only 29 per cent still had it on sixty days later. Tanvi had grown up on a grape farm, and she had a personal reason to look further. Her father owned a Nirjhar controller and had switched the automatic schedule off in his first season. “The app does not know my vines,” he had said. On the dashboard he was an active user.

So, over two weekends, Tanvi went to see for herself. With two of Rajesh’s technicians, who knew the roads, she visited thirty farms in two districts, growing grapes, onions, pomegranates and sugarcane, all of them counted as active users. She also phoned six dealers. She told nobody, because nobody had asked her to.

Phase B · Thirty farms · 3 of 6
“Then we agree.”

Tanvi wrote a one-page note. Unsure whom to send it to, she emailed it to Anjali, “because it is about the app”. She copied no one. Anjali read it late at night and was irritated before she had finished. The next morning she took it to Prakash’s office, and he read it standing up.

AN

PJ
Prakash“Quite right. And if anybody wants to suggest that the dealers are the problem, I would want proper market research, not an analyst’s weekend visits.”

Prakash“We do.”

They did not discuss what, if anything, should happen next, or whose job it would be. Later that week, at a dinner, each told a colleague privately that the real problem lay in the other’s area.

Fourteen years, always cordial

The two had worked together for fourteen years and were always cordial. Two years earlier, Prakash had promised a large dealer group a remote pump-start feature before the season, without asking R&D. Anjali’s team had worked nights to deliver it. She had said nothing at the time. Since then she had been a little slower to answer his emails.

Eight of Tanvi’s thirty farmers open the app only to start the pump from home.

Phase C · The Monday meeting · 1 of 6
Every feature worked, and it would pass

By late November the demonstration was ready. On a large screen, a farmer could ask Saathi in Marathi, “Should I water today?” and hear an answer within two seconds. The new dashboard was handsome. Every feature worked. It would impress the Board, it would look good on stage in Pune, and it would pass.

That weekend, two messages reached Aditya.

Mr Kale, Chairman, by telephone

“The Board must see that we are ahead of the start-ups. The dealers’ meet cannot slip.”

Every line on the left is partly right. The difference is what happens next.
Carol Dweck calls these a fixed and a growth mindset. Chris Argyris would call the habit on the left defensive reasoning: keeping our reasoning private and our conclusions untested. The case never used any of these words. The next pages look at Nirjhar through both lenses.
Debrief · Advantages you can see
Where did each one show in the case?

Before you click, find the moment in the case yourself.

Dweck (2016) reports that where whole companies embrace a growth mindset, employees feel far more empowered and committed and get more support for collaboration and innovation. In mainly fixed-mindset companies, people report more cheating and deception.
Argyris lens · 1 of 6 · single and double loop
A controller that asks why it is set at six
Single loop

Too cold? Turn the heat on. Warm enough? Turn it off.

Double loop

“Why am I set at 68 degrees?” Is there a better way to heat the room?

Argyris (1991): highly skilled professionals are often very good at single-loop learning and poor at double-loop learning, because they have rarely failed, and so have never learned how to learn from failure.

Argyris lens · 2 of 6 · the learning dilemma
Why smart, committed people stop learning

Argyris spent fifteen years studying bright, highly committed management consultants. They were enthusiastic about improving everything around them. The moment improvement turned to their own performance, they felt embarrassed and threatened, and they put the blame on unclear goals, unfair leaders and difficult clients.

His point: this is not a problem of attitude or motivation. It is a problem of how people reason, a “master program” most of us run without noticing.

At Nirjhar, the controller “never breaks” and the company had rarely failed. Then a note arrived. Anjali was “irritated before she had finished”.
The four values behind defensive reasoning
1Stay in unilateral control
2Maximise winning, minimise losing
3Suppress negative feelings
4Be as “rational” as possible: set clear objectives and judge yourself only by whether you hit them

Their shared purpose, says Argyris, is to avoid embarrassment or threat. Our espoused theory may say “continuous improvement”; our theory-in-use says “protect yourself”. When high achievers do fall short, some go into what his consultants called the doom loop.

    Questions and probes
    • Why a company case. Nobody in the room runs an irrigation business, so nobody needs to defend one. The link back to their own institutions is made at the end.

    Quality check
    1. Each phase ends on a commitment, and Phase C ends on four options with real costs.
