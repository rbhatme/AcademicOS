# The Demo That Passed

::: {custom-style="Small Note"}
Fictional case. Read Phase A only. Do not turn the page until the facilitator asks you to.

## Phase A: The Board's minute

Nirjhar Agro Systems had a good name in the fields of western Maharashtra and knew it. Founded twenty-seven years earlier by Madhukar Kale, it made drip-irrigation controllers, valves and solar pumps, with annual sales of about ₹420 crore and 850 employees. Its controllers ran on some 2.1 lakh farms, sold and serviced through 600 dealers in five states. Farmers said a Nirjhar controller "never breaks", and its field service was the best in the region. Growth, though, had slowed from eight per cent a year to five, and two well-funded agritech start-ups had begun selling cheap soil sensors with a free advisory app. Nobody at Nirjhar thought the company was in trouble. A few people, privately, thought it had stopped moving.

Aditya Mahajan had been Managing Director for nine months. Before that he had spent sixteen years at Nirjhar, the last six as Head of Operations, so he knew every plant and every member of the leadership team. In his first months he mostly listened. One question he asked everyone was, "What does a farmer actually buy from us?" Most people answered with a list of products. A few said "a better crop, with less water and less worry". He noticed the difference and kept it to himself.

In late October the Board met. Mr Kale, now Chairman, was worried about the start-ups. Ritu Sengupta, an independent director from the technology industry, asked the question everyone expected: what was Nirjhar doing about artificial intelligence? The Board minuted a request (Exhibit 1). Management was to present an AI product strategy, with a working demonstration, at the Board meeting on 12 December. The minute ended: "The Board wishes to understand how AI can change what a farmer gets from us, so that we can learn quickly." Aditya forwarded it to his team with one line: "Let us discuss on Monday."

Seven people met in the MD's office. Dr Anjali Menon, Head of R&D, spoke first, and she was relaxed. "Honestly, we already have AI. Saathi has been scheduling irrigation from weather forecasts for three years, and last season we trained a model on sensor data from forty thousand farms. Add a voice assistant in Marathi and a new dashboard, and the Board has its demonstration in four weeks." Anjali had led Nirjhar's engineering for fourteen years. The controller that never broke was hers, and so was the Saathi app. Mr Kale introduced her at every dealers' meet as "the reason we sleep at night".

Prakash Jadhav, Head of Sales and the Dealer Network, agreed. He had built the network over twenty years, dealer by dealer. "Dealers have been asking for AI on the brochure since the start-ups arrived. Give them something to show at the meet. Leave the farmers to us. Our dealers know every farmer by name."

Neha Agarwal, Head of Marketing, had booked the dealers' meet in Pune for 8 January and asked only that the demonstration look good on stage under the line "AI for every acre". Imran Sayyed, the CFO, asked whether any new spending was involved. "If it is a software upgrade, it is not really a finance matter. Just do not ask me for a data science team this year." Rajesh Patil, Head of Field Service, who managed 120 technicians, asked for two weeks' notice before any launch so that he could train them. Nobody thought it was more than a demonstration.

Aditya listened. This was a capable team, loyal to the company and respectful of one another. When he asked for something, it got done. He also had a feeling he could not quite put into words: that things got done exactly as asked, and no further. He noticed that nobody had asked what the Board meant by "learn quickly", or what a farmer would do differently because of the new product.

At the end of the table sat Tanvi Shinde, an analyst on the small Farmer Insights desk in Marketing. She had joined fourteen months earlier from an agricultural NGO, and was there only to show one slide of app figures. She said nothing.

"So we are ready," said Anjali, closing her laptop.

"Demonstration to me by 25 November," said Aditya.

## Phase B: Thirty farms

Tanvi's task, from Neha, was to "pull the app numbers for the demo slide". The numbers were good (Exhibit 1): 1.2 lakh downloads and 46,000 monthly active users, up thirty per cent in a year. But the same data showed something the dashboard did not. Of the 31,000 farms that had switched on Saathi's automatic schedule, only 29 per cent still had it on sixty days later. Tanvi had grown up on a grape farm, and she had a personal reason to look further. Her father owned a Nirjhar controller and had switched the automatic schedule off in his first season. "The app does not know my vines," he had said. On the dashboard he was an active user.

So, over two weekends, Tanvi went to see for herself. With two of Rajesh's technicians, who knew the roads, she visited thirty farms in two districts, growing grapes, onions, pomegranates and sugarcane, all of them counted as active users. She asked each farmer the same three questions. Do you use the automatic schedule? What would make you trust it? What do you wish the company knew about your farm? She also phoned six dealers. She told nobody, because nobody had asked her to.

Her notes (Exhibit 2) were not what anyone expected. Seven of the thirty farmers used the automatic schedule regularly. Fifteen had tried it and gone back to manual. Eight had never switched it on; they opened the app only to start the pump from home. The most common reason surprised her: on fourteen farms the schedule clashed with the hours when the farm had electricity, which rotated between day and night from week to week. Others said the app never explained its advice. Eighteen farmers asked, without being prompted, for an alert when the pump failed or the power came back. Five asked for voice advice in Marathi, the feature at the centre of the demonstration. One dealer said, "Give me something that tells me which farmer's pump is about to fail, and I will sell it tomorrow."

Tanvi wrote a one-page note. Unsure whom to send it to, she emailed it to Anjali, "because it is about the app". She copied no one.

Anjali read it late at night and was irritated before she had finished. The next morning she took it to Prakash's office, and he read it standing up. The two had worked together for fourteen years and were always cordial. Two years earlier, Prakash had promised a large dealer group a remote pump-start feature before the season, without asking R&D. Anjali's team had worked nights to deliver it. She had said nothing at the time. Since then she had been a little slower to answer his emails.

"Thirty farms," Anjali said. "That is a story, not data. The model has learned from forty thousand. Farmers go back to manual because they always have. Adoption takes time. That is a dealer-training matter, not a product matter."

"Quite right," said Prakash. "And if anybody wants to suggest that the dealers are the problem, I would want proper market research, not an analyst's weekend visits."

"Then we agree," said Anjali.

"We do," said Prakash. They did not discuss what, if anything, should happen next, or whose job it would be. Later that week, at a dinner, each told a colleague privately that the real problem lay in the other's area.

The note travelled. Neha heard of it and said quietly to Aditya, "If this gets out before the dealers' meet, the start-ups will quote it in every village." Imran pointed out that proper farmer research and a data team would cost about ₹2.5 crore a year, which this year's budget did not have. Rajesh admitted that for more than a year his technicians had been switching farms back to manual whenever farmers complained, because it ended the complaints. Nobody had asked him, so he had not mentioned it. All three were right.

Aditya saw the note on Friday, when Rajesh forwarded it "for information". He read it twice. He had no way of knowing whether thirty farms were typical. He did know that he had read the Saathi dashboard every month for three years, and that it had never contained a sentence like "the app does not know when the power comes".

## Phase C: The Monday meeting

By late November the demonstration was ready. On a large screen, a farmer could ask Saathi in Marathi, "Should I water today?" and hear an answer within two seconds. The new dashboard was handsome. Every feature worked. It would impress the Board, it would look good on stage in Pune, and it would pass.

That weekend, two messages reached Aditya. Mr Kale telephoned: "The Board must see that we are ahead of the start-ups. The dealers' meet cannot slip." On Sunday evening Ritu Sengupta sent a short email: "I do not need a demo. I want to know what we have learned about farmers." She had not seen Tanvi's note.

On Monday morning Aditya called Tanvi in. She was nervous. "Did I go too far? I visited farms without asking anyone."

"No," said Aditya. "But I do not yet know what you have started."

The leadership team would meet at eleven. Aditya wanted to leave the meeting with a decision, not, as he put it, "a discussion about whether to discuss". The Board would meet on 12 December, the dealers on 8 January, and the summer irrigation season, when most controllers were sold, began in February. In his notebook he had written four options.

::: {custom-style="Commit Box"}
**Predict before you read the options.** What will this team most probably decide on Monday, and why? Write one line.
:::

**Option A. Launch as planned, and log the finding.** Nirjhar shows the Board the demonstration on 12 December and launches the AI adviser at the dealers' meet on 8 January, in all five states. Tanvi's note goes into the product backlog, and Aditya asks Anjali and Prakash to review, after the summer season, whether changes such as the farm's power hours are needed. *What it offers:* the company keeps its commitments to the Board and the dealers, gives the dealers an answer to the start-ups, protects the selling season, and puts no department on trial on the strength of thirty farm visits. *What it costs:* the finding rests in the backlog. Thousands of farmers meet an adviser that many have already switched off, and if it gives bad advice in grape season, the controller's name suffers with it. The people who noticed learn what happens to inconvenient news.

**Option B. Show the Board on time, and launch as a 90-day pilot in two districts.** Nirjhar shows the demonstration on 12 December with a one-page note: the adviser will launch in two districts first, with the farm's power hours added, and will be measured for ninety days on three measures agreed in advance, such as whether farmers keep the schedule switched on. The Board decides on a full launch in April. A small team of one R&D engineer, one regional sales manager, one of Rajesh's technicians and Tanvi runs it. *What it offers:* learning at small cost and small risk, evidence instead of anecdote, and something real to announce at the dealers' meet. *What it costs:* dealers outside the two districts are disappointed and the start-ups gain a season. Busy people must find time at the busiest time of year, it is not clear who leads a team that crosses four departments, and a pilot that finds problems will have to be acted on.

**Option C. Redesign the adviser with the people closest to the farm.** Nirjhar gives the Board on 12 December an honest account of what it has learned and a plan, and shows the adviser at the dealers' meet as a preview only. Before Christmas the leadership team spends two days visiting farms together, and the company holds a two-day working session with fifteen farmers, ten dealers, eight technicians and the R&D team. The aim is to agree what the adviser must know about a farm, and who in the company owns what farmers say. *What it offers:* ownership well beyond the leadership team, a product farmers may actually trust, and the largest possible improvement. *What it costs:* it is slow and visible. The summer season may pass without a new product, weaknesses will be discussed in front of dealers and farmers, the Chairman may be displeased, and expectations may rise beyond what the budget can meet.

**Option D. Ask the Board to decide, with outside help.** Nirjhar sends the Board the demonstration together with Tanvi's note, and asks whether to launch, whether to commission an outside agritech consultancy to study farmer adoption across all five states, and who should own AI in the company. *What it offers:* the Board's authority, outside expertise in a field that is new to Nirjhar, and a decision that does not rest on any one department. The start-ups are, after all, a strategic threat. *What it costs:* the decision, and with it the ownership, moves out of the leadership team. It is slower, the consultants learn what the company needed to learn, and it may be read as a team that could not decide.

# Teaching note (for the facilitator only)

## Purpose

This is the first session of a fortnightly series for the senior leaders of an education society. It aims to bring two behaviours to the surface without naming or judging them: carrying out a task without asking what it is for ("we already do this"), and cordial relations that stop short of shared ownership ("that is not my department"). The case is set in a company on purpose. Nobody in the room runs an irrigation business, so nobody needs to defend one; the link back to their own institutions is made at the end. One session will not change habits. It should open questions the series can build on.

## Before the session

- **Group.** 12 to 24 people, seated in groups of four to six. For more than 24, add table reporters.
- **Print.** Phase A, Phase B, Phase C with options, and the exhibits as separate handouts, or use the interactive file. Give each person the participant pack at the start, with the Mirror sheet on top.
- **Role cards** for Phase B: Aditya, Anjali, Prakash, Neha, Imran, Rajesh, Tanvi. Give each person a role far from their own job. For example, a finance head plays Tanvi and an academic dean plays Prakash.
- **Ground rules, said aloud.** The case is fictional. No one's institution, department or performance is being reviewed. Argue positions, not people. Seniority does not decide the answer. Nobody is asked which character they resemble.

## Phase A: questions and probes

1. *What did the Board ask for, and what did it say it wanted?* The minute asks for a demonstration, a plan and a budget; its last sentence asks to "learn quickly". Put both on the board.
2. *Anjali says "we already have AI". Is she right?* Partly, and say so: a model trained on forty thousand farms is real work. Probe: "What does the dashboard measure, and what does it not measure?" "How would Nirjhar know if farmers were not being helped?"
3. *What did Aditya notice, and what did nobody ask?* Draw out "exactly as asked, and no further", and the unasked question of what a farmer would do differently.

**If you hear "we already do this" about real practice:** agree with what is true, then ask "What evidence would show it is working? What would a newcomer question?" Never correct it as wrong. The point is curiosity, not blame.

## Phase B: questions and probes

1. *In role: what is your character worried about, and what is your character right about?* Every character is right about something. Make sure each gets said.
2. *Anjali and Prakash "agree". What did they agree on, and what did they avoid?* They agreed the finding was weak. They avoided what to do and whose job it was. The pump-start story shows the cost of a disagreement that was never voiced; note the irony that the feature born of that friction is the one many farmers actually use.
3. *Rajesh's technicians switched farms to manual for a year.* Was that a good decision? Whose decision was it? Why did nobody hear? This is the clearest hidden problem in the case, and it came from helpful people.
4. *A model trained on forty thousand farms, and thirty conversations. What does each know that the other does not?* Data shows what farmers do; conversations show why. Neither alone is enough.
5. If someone says Tanvi overstepped, ask: "What do we want her to do next time? What will she actually do next time?"

## Phase C: options

Take the prediction poll first; most rooms predict Option A. Then poll the options. Ask one advocate per option for the strongest case, then ask the same person for the strongest case against it.

- **A.** On time and safe, and competitive timing is real. *What does it teach Tanvi and Rajesh?* A is defensible if the review is real, has a date and has an owner.
- **B.** *Who leads? What would make the pilot fail? How would we treat a pilot that brings bad news?*
- **C.** *What does visiting farms together change? What is the risk, and who carries it?* This echoes Microsoft's executives from different businesses visiting customers together during their retreat.
- **D.** *When is outside help a way to learn, and when is it a way to hand the learning to someone else?* D has a real case: the start-ups are a strategic matter for the Board.

There is no right answer. The learning is in what each person needed to believe to choose their option.

## Mirror reveal and debrief

Ask everyone to open the sealed sheet and complete section 4 of the pack. Share one gap of your own first. Invite two or three volunteers; do not call on anyone. Typical gaps: rating S2 at 5 and choosing A; rating S3 at 5 while, in role, agreeing with Prakash in the corridor. Chris Argyris called this the difference between the theory we espouse and the theory we use.

Now name the idea for the first time: the difference between a mindset that protects what we already know and one that treats every task as a chance to learn (Carol Dweck's "fixed" and "growth" mindsets). Use the case to show the **advantages** of the second:

| Advantage | Where it showed in the case |
|---|---|
| Faster, cheaper learning | Two weekends and thirty farms found what three years of dashboards had not |
| Ownership beyond the brief | Tanvi was asked for a slide and went after the purpose |
| Using the people closest to the problem | Farmers, dealers and technicians held the evidence; technicians had acted on it for a year |
| Fewer hidden problems | The dashboard counted switched-off farms as active users, and Rajesh's quiet fix stayed hidden because nobody asked |
| Constructive disagreement | Anjali and Prakash's polite agreement avoided the real question |
| Credibility with customers | A dealer would sell a pump-failure alert tomorrow: listening finds products, not only faults |
| Resilience when things change | Start-ups, power hours and AI itself change every season; a learning team adjusts, a demo is just repeated |
| Keeping and growing talent | What Tanvi learns now decides what the next Tanvi does |

**Be honest about the limits.**

- Thirty farms do not prove the model wrong. Anjali's demand for real data is legitimate, and rigour is part of learning.
- Speed matters too. Competitors are real, and sometimes launching on time is the right call.
- A growth mindset is not positivity or praising effort alone.
- It must not become a new label for criticising colleagues. The Microsoft case describes Nadella stopping exactly this.
- On when to be firm: hold firm when the evidence is strong or ethics, safety or customers' interests are at stake. Accommodate when a decision is low-cost and reversible. Help beyond your role when the organisation's goal is at risk and your help is cheap compared with the cost to others.

## Bridge: Nadella, Argyris, and from the farm to the campus (10 minutes)

Use your own words. The facts below are from the London Business School case (Ibarra, Rattan and Johnston, 2018). Quote no more than shown.

- Satya Nadella became Microsoft's CEO in February 2014, an insider of 22 years, like Aditya. Microsoft was very profitable, but its own employees described a culture of internal fights and inertia.
- He spent much of his first year listening, and asked why the company existed.
- He drew on Carol Dweck's research and described the shift as moving from "know-it-alls" to "learn-it-alls".
- By 2018 the case describes Microsoft's strategic bets on AI and cloud computing as paying off. The culture change came first.
- He did not impose a definition. Kathleen Hogan and 180 executives in 17 teams worked out what the idea would mean for Microsoft.
- He wanted the senior team to treat itself as each member's "first team, not just another meeting they attended".
- At the executive retreat, executives from different businesses were sent together to visit customers, despite "eye-rolling and groaning". In Austria, one account manager spent a week with police officers and another two days in a hospital, to see a customer's problem first-hand. Tanvi did the same on two weekends.
- When he made a public mistake, he told all employees he had "answered that question completely wrong".
- Leaders closed meetings by asking whether the meeting had been growth-minded or fixed-minded, and why.

**Argyris (1991).** In "Teaching Smart People How to Learn", Argyris argued that highly capable professionals often fall back on defensive reasoning when learning is needed, because they have rarely had to learn from failure.

**From the farm to the campus.** Ask three questions and let tables answer for their own area, not anyone else's: *Who are our farmers? What is our Saathi dashboard, the number that looks good and that nobody checks with the people it is for? Who is our Tanvi, and what would happen to her note?*

## Close and link to session 2

Use the meeting-close question from the Microsoft case: "Was this a learning discussion or a defending discussion? Why?" Each person writes two commitments (pack, section 5). Collect the anonymous "demo that passed" slips; they are the raw material for the series.

For session 2, each participant brings one current initiative from their own area. The questions will be: what is it for, and how would you improve it? The institute case, "The Report That Passed", can then be used to bring the same questions home.

# Quality check and sources to verify

**Quality check**

1. Each phase ends on a decision or a commitment, and Phase C ends on four options.
2. No option is presented as best. Each has a stated advantage and a stated cost, and the comfortable Option A is defensible.
3. Every character is right about something: Anjali (the model is real work, and thirty farms are not data), Prakash (dealers know farmers, and commitments matter), Neha (competitors will use any weakness), Imran (research and data teams cost money), Rajesh (switching to manual served farmers), Tanvi (the AI does not know the farm), Mr Kale (the start-ups are a real threat) and Ritu Sengupta (learning is the point).
4. The case text and participant pack never name the concept. It is named only in the debrief.
5. The company, people, products and figures are invented, and the product is kept generic. Check with one farmer or dealer that rotating day and night power hours for farm pumps is described plausibly for your region.
