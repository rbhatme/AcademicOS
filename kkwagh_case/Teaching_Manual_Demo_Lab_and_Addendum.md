**How to use this manual.** One row per page: what a good answer looks like, and how to read what you hear. Most pages have no single right answer. Where a file shows "One reading", use it as a benchmark to discuss, never as a mark. Participants write before they discuss; sealed pages enforce this (press **U** to open all seals when projecting). Keys: → or Space next, ← back, D theme, F full screen, M menu, R twice clears. Nothing is stored, so participants must copy or print their pack before closing.

# Part A. Learning Lab

## Start and Phase A

| # | Page | Appropriate answers | How to interpret · your move |
|---|----------|----------------------------|----------------------|
| 1 | Welcome | None. Read the ground rules aloud. | Stress: fictional case, nobody is being assessed. |
| 2 | Mirror sheet | Most leaders rate S1 to S3 at 4 or 5. | No right rating. Do not comment; the ratings matter only at the reveal (p.19). |
| 3 | A good name | "A few people thought it had stopped moving." Aditya's question: products versus purpose. | Ask who in the room answers "what does our department exist for" with activities. |
| 4 | The Board's minute | The minute asks for a demo, a plan and a budget, and also to "learn quickly". The dashboard measures use, not benefit. | Put "what is asked" against "what is wanted" on the board. |
| 5 | Monday's meeting | Anjali spoke first; Tanvi said nothing; nobody asked what "learn quickly" meant or what farmers would do differently. | Good groups notice the silence, not only the speakers. |
| 6 | Commit: Phase A | Q1: any honest sentence (often "we already have it"). Q2: **partly**: real AI, but a strategy needs purpose, farmer outcomes and evidence of use. Q3: understand what farmers need and learn from evidence fast. | "Yes, fully" at Q2 signals single-loop thinking; do not correct it, probe: "how would we know it helps farmers?" |

## Argyris lens

| # | Page | Appropriate answers | How to interpret · your move |
|---|----------|----------------------------|----------------------|
| 22 | Two loops | Single loop adjusts the action; double loop questions the goal ("why water at 6 a.m. when power comes at 2 a.m.?"). Good "your own" example: goal "results by May"; second loop "why judge a year by one exam?" | If a participant's second loop is only a better action, it is still single-loop. |
| 23 | The learning dilemma | Four values: unilateral control; win, do not lose; suppress negative feelings; look rational. | Smart people rarely fail, so they never learn how to. |
| 24 | Spot the four values | One reading: 1 Control, 2 Control, 3 Winning, 4 Winning, 5 Hiding feelings, 6 Hiding feelings, 7 Looking rational, 8 Hiding feelings. | Not scored; most moments carry two values. Spotting the pattern matters more than the label. |
| 25 | Rewrite four lines | A productive rewrite names the limit, keeps the data, makes the inference explicit, proposes a test and says what would change the speaker's mind. | Compare with the model; ask what the productive version risked. |
| 26 | Aditya's left column | The strongest unsaid thought to voice: "I have read this dashboard for three years and never asked what it does not show." | Like Argyris's CEO, what Aditya hides comes through anyway. |
| 27 | Your left column | A good test sentence turns an assumption into a question, e.g. "I am assuming you see this as the students' problem; is that right?" | Private. Encourage, do not collect. |

**Reading the room: five signals.** A large gap between prediction and choice (Lab p.17) means stated ambition outruns practice. High Mirror ratings with option A (p.19) are the classic espoused-versus-in-use gap. "Nobody's problem" at the Phase B commit means ownership is the issue to work on. A low policy average (p.34) points to slogans without systems. Readings that the room rejects with good reasons are a sign of a learning discussion, not a failed one.

# Part B. Addendum, Since 1991

Use 15 to 20 minutes after the lab, or as reading before session 2. Take one development per session. Every claim carries a label: **well established**, **still developing**, **early evidence**, **contested or overstated**.

| # | Page | Appropriate answers | How to interpret · your move |
|---|----------|----------------------------|----------------------|
| 1 | Since 1991 | Timeline from Argyris (1991) to generative AI (2025 to 2026). | Explain the four evidence labels first. |
| 2 | Five extensions | Double-loop → triple loop, AI. Defensive reasoning and espoused versus in-use → safety, cognitive science. Doom loop → failure literacy, safety. Tough reasoning → cognitive science, AI, failure. Change at the top → safety, triple loop. | Shows the five are one argument, not five topics. |
| 3 | Climate between heads | "Then we agree" → comfort zone. "Did I go too far?" → anxiety zone. Pilot with agreed measures → learning zone. Heads down → apathy zone. | Most cordial leadership teams sit in the comfort zone. Learning needs both safety and high standards. |
| 4 | Safety is not niceness | Best response to Tanvi: the third ("Thank you... what surprised you most... what would we need to test it?"). The case's own reply is honest but incomplete; the first and last close her down. | Map it to Edmondson's toolkit: set the stage, invite participation, respond productively. |
| 5 | A third loop | Third-loop question: who decides what counts as success, and whose voice is missing? (Nirjhar: farmers and technicians; college: students and employers.) | If answers stay at "is the goal right?", it is still the second loop. |
| 6 | What the review found | Five processes: changing structure, participative leadership, reciprocal power, learning culture, transparent information. Outcomes: shared knowledge, a new system, adaptability. | Note the caveat: no agreed definition. Use it as a question, not a model. |
| 7 | Not all failures are equal | Preventable, complex, intelligent. Four criteria for intelligent failure: new territory, real goal, hypothesis, small. | Each type needs a different response: correct, analyse, thank. |
| 8 | Sort eight failures | One reading: 1 Preventable, 2 Complex, 3 Intelligent, 4 Preventable, 5 Intelligent, 6 Complex, 7 Intelligent, 8 Preventable. | Matches are not a score. Differences open the question "what would we need to know?" |
| 9 | Ladder of inference | Anjali climbs from 30 farms to "launch as planned" in seven rungs, skipping the 14 power clashes. A good own example names the data at the bottom. | Climbing down means asking the rung's question, not defending the conclusion. |
| 10 | Evidence check | Confirmation bias: solid. System 1/2 link: a useful parallel only. Identity-protective cognition: established, extended. "Threat like physical danger": overstated. Ego depletion: contested. | A model of how leaders should treat popular claims: ask for the evidence. |
| 11 | AI learning paradox | Answer machine: the inner loop speeds up and the outer fades. Thinking partner: both loops turn. | Link to Saathi: fluent answers built on an unexamined premise (power hours). |
| 12 | What the evidence says | Six studies, labelled. The pattern: how people use AI matters more than how often. | Correct anyone who cites the unverified "AI Learning Paradox" study. |
| 13 | Friction by design | Strong sets include: write your own view first; ask what the answer assumes about our context; check one claim against our data. | Four rules at most; review in 30 days. |
| 14 | LOOP, updated | L adds triple loop; O adds ladder and AI premises; Own adds psychological safety; P adds intelligent failure. | Ties the addendum back to the lab. |
| 15 | Three experiments | A safety move (thank, ask, next step); a failure, typed; one AI rule. | Specific beats general. Copy or print before closing. |
| 16 | Sources and corrections | Five corrections to the original summary; full references. | Read before the session. |
