Phase A · The Board’s minute · 1 of 4
A good name in the fields, and it knew it

Nirjhar Agro Systems had a good name in the fields of western Maharashtra and knew it. Founded twenty-seven years earlier by Madhukar Kale, it made drip-irrigation controllers, valves and solar pumps. Farmers said a Nirjhar controller “never breaks”, and its field service was the best in the region. Growth, though, had slowed from eight per cent a year to five, and two well-funded agritech start-ups had begun selling cheap soil sensors with a free advisory app. Nobody at Nirjhar thought the company was in trouble. A few people, privately, thought it had stopped moving.

Aditya Mahajan had been Managing Director for nine months. Before that he had spent sixteen years at Nirjhar, the last six as Head of Operations, so he knew every plant and every member of the leadership team. In his first months he mostly listened. One question he asked everyone was, “What does a farmer actually buy from us?” Most people answered with a list of products. A few said “a better crop, with less water and less worry”. He noticed the difference and kept it to himself.

Nirjhar at a glance
₹420crore in annual sales, with 850 employees
2.1lakh farms running a Nirjhar controller
600dealers in five states
8→5per cent a year: growth slowing as start-ups arrive

“So that we can learn quickly”

In late October the Board met. Mr Kale, now Chairman, was worried about the start-ups. Ritu Sengupta, an independent director from the technology industry, asked the question everyone expected: what was Nirjhar doing about artificial intelligence?

Phase B · Thirty farms · 1 of 6
“The app does not know my vines”

Tanvi’s task, from Neha, was to “pull the app numbers for the demo slide”. The numbers were good: 1.2 lakh downloads and 46,000 monthly active users, up thirty per cent in a year. But the same data showed something the dashboard did not. Of the 31,000 farms that had switched on Saathi’s automatic schedule, only 29 per cent still had it on sixty days later. Tanvi had grown up on a grape farm, and she had a personal reason to look further. Her father owned a Nirjhar controller and had switched the automatic schedule off in his first season. “The app does not know my vines,” he had said. On the dashboard he was an active user.

So, over two weekends, Tanvi went to see for herself. With two of Rajesh’s technicians, who knew the roads, she visited thirty farms in two districts, growing grapes, onions, pomegranates and sugarcane, all of them counted as active users. She also phoned six dealers. She told nobody, because nobody had asked her to.

Phase B · Thirty farms · 3 of 6
“Then we agree.”

Tanvi wrote a one-page note. Unsure whom to send it to, she emailed it to Anjali, “because it is about the app”. She copied no one. Anjali read it late at night and was irritated before she had finished. The next morning she took it to Prakash’s office, and he read it standing up.

AN

PJ
Prakash“Quite right. And if anybody wants to suggest that the dealers are the problem, I would want proper market research, not an analyst’s weekend visits.”

Prakash“We do.”

They did not discuss what, if anything, should happen next, or whose job it would be. Later that week, at a dinner, each told a colleague privately that the real problem lay in the other’s area.

Fourteen years, always cordial

The two had worked together for fourteen years and were always cordial. Two years earlier, Prakash had promised a large dealer group a remote pump-start feature before the season, without asking R&D. Anjali’s team had worked nights to deliver it. She had said nothing at the time. Since then she had been a little slower to answer his emails.

Eight of Tanvi’s thirty farmers open the app only to start the pump from home.

Phase C · The Monday meeting · 1 of 6
Every feature worked, and it would pass

By late November the demonstration was ready. On a large screen, a farmer could ask Saathi in Marathi, “Should I water today?” and hear an answer within two seconds. The new dashboard was handsome. Every feature worked. It would impress the Board, it would look good on stage in Pune, and it would pass.

That weekend, two messages reached Aditya.

Mr Kale, Chairman, by telephone

“The Board must see that we are ahead of the start-ups. The dealers’ meet cannot slip.”

Ritu Sengupta, independent director, Sunday email

“I do not need a demo. I want to know what we have learned about farmers.”

Ritu Sengupta had not seen Tanvi’s note.

Debrief · Name the idea
Two ways to meet the same Board minute
Protecting what we already knowA fixed mindset
Treating the task as a chance to learnA growth mindset
“Honestly, we already have AI.”
“We have part of it. What did the Board mean by ‘learn quickly’?”
“Give them something to show at the meet.”
“Give them something to show, and find out whether farmers use it.”
“Thirty farms. That is a story, not data.”
“Thirty farms is a signal, not proof. What would real evidence look like, and who would gather it?”
“Then we agree.” (And at dinner, it is the other area’s problem.)
“We disagree about where the problem lies. Let us settle it here.”
“If it is a software upgrade, it is not really a finance matter.”
“What would a small test cost, and can finance help to size it?”
Every line on the left is partly right. The difference is what happens next.
Carol Dweck’s research calls these a fixed and a growth mindset: one protects what we already know, the other treats every task as a chance to learn. The case never used either phrase. You found the difference before it had a name.

Debrief · Honest limits
What this idea is not
Rigour is part of learning

Thirty farms do not prove the model wrong. Anjali’s demand for real data is legitimate.

Speed matters too

Competitors are real, and sometimes launching on time is the right call.

Not positivity, not praise for effort alone

Saying the right words while nothing changes is its own trap (“false growth mindset”).

Not a new label for criticising colleagues

Bridge · why smart people defend, and what it means for us
From the farm to the campus

In “Teaching Smart People How to Learn” (Harvard Business Review, 1991), Chris Argyris argued that highly capable professionals often fall back on defensive reasoning just when learning is needed, because they have rarely had to learn from failure.

Success makes a team good at doing what it already does. It does not make the team good at finding out what it is missing.

Facilitator · what to ask, what you may hear
Questions and probes
  • Why a company case. Nobody in the room runs an irrigation business, so nobody needs to defend one. The link back to their own institutions is made at the end.
  • Role cards for Phase B: give each person a role far from their own job.
  • Ground rules, said aloud. The case is fictional. No one’s institution, department or performance is being reviewed. Argue positions, not people. Seniority does not decide the answer. Nobody is asked which character they resemble.
  • Silence after a question is thinking time. Wait, then ask the table to share what they wrote, not what they think now.
  1. What did the Board ask for, and what did it say it wanted? The minute asks for a demonstration, a plan and a budget; its last sentence asks to “learn quickly”. Put both on the board.
  2. Anjali says “we already have AI”. Is she right? Partly, and say so: a model trained on forty thousand farms is real work. Probe: “What does the dashboard measure, and what does it not measure?” “How would Nirjhar know if farmers were not being helped?”
  3. What did Aditya notice, and what did nobody ask? Draw out “exactly as asked, and no further”, and the unasked question of what a farmer would do differently.

If you hear “we already do this” about real practice: agree with what is true, then ask “What evidence would show it is working? What would a newcomer question?” Never correct it as wrong. The point is curiosity, not blame.

  1. In role: what is your character worried about, and what is your character right about? Make sure each gets said.
  2. Anjali and Prakash “agree”. What did they agree on, and what did they avoid? They agreed the finding was weak and avoided what to do and whose job it was. The pump-start story shows the cost of a disagreement never voiced; the feature born of that friction is the one many farmers actually use.
  3. Rajesh’s technicians switched farms to manual for a year. Was that a good decision? Whose was it? Why did nobody hear? The clearest hidden problem in the case came from helpful people.
  4. Forty thousand farms of data, and thirty conversations. What does each know that the other does not? Data shows what farmers do; conversations show why.
  5. If someone says Tanvi overstepped, ask: “What do we want her to do next time? What will she actually do next time?”

Take the prediction poll first; most rooms predict Option A. Then poll the options. Ask one advocate per option for the strongest case, then ask the same person for the strongest case against it.

  • A. On time and safe, and competitive timing is real. What does it teach Tanvi and Rajesh? A is defensible if the review is real, has a date and has an owner.
  • B. Who leads? What would make the pilot fail? How would we treat a pilot that brings bad news?
  • C. What does visiting farms together change? What is the risk, and who carries it? This echoes Microsoft’s executives from different businesses visiting customers together during their retreat.
  • D. When is outside help a way to learn, and when is it a way to hand the learning to someone else? D has a real case: the start-ups are a strategic matter for the Board.

There is no right answer. The learning is in what each person needed to believe to choose their option.

  1. Everyone opens the sealed sheet. Share one gap of your own first. Invite two or three volunteers; do not call on anyone.
  2. Typical gaps: rating S2 at 5 and choosing A; rating S3 at 5 while, in role, agreeing with Prakash in the corridor.
  3. Name the idea for the first time, then walk the advantages: ask the room to find each in the case before you reveal it.
  4. Be honest about the limits, and use the firm, accommodate or help page for the second behaviour: cordial relations that stop short of shared ownership.
  5. From the farm to the campus: who are our farmers, what is our dashboard, who is our Tanvi? Let tables answer for their own area.
Facilitator · before the session
Quality check and sources to verify
Quality check
  1. Each phase ends on a decision or a commitment, and Phase C ends on four options.
  2. No option is presented as best. Each has a stated advantage and a stated cost, and the comfortable Option A is defensible.
  3. Every character is right about something: Anjali (the model is real work, and thirty farms are not data), Prakash (dealers know farmers, and commitments matter), Neha (competitors will use any weakness), Imran (research costs money), Rajesh (switching to manual served farmers), Tanvi (the AI does not know the farm), Mr Kale (the start-ups are a real threat) and Ritu Sengupta (learning is the point).
  4. The case text never names the concept. It is named only in the debrief.
  5. The company, people, products and figures are invented, and the product is kept generic. Check with one farmer or dealer that rotating day and night power hours for farm pumps is described plausibly for your region.
