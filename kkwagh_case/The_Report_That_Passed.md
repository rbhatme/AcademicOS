# The Report That Passed

::: {custom-style="Small Note"}
Fictional case. Read Phase A only. Do not turn the page until the facilitator asks you to.

## Phase A: The circular

The Aruna Institute of Technology and Management (AITM) had a good reputation and knew it. Its 3,300 students studied engineering, management and computer applications, and about 1,100 graduated each year. For six years running its placement figures had been the best in the district. Applications were steady, results were respectable, and the accreditation visit two years earlier had gone well. Nobody at AITM thought the Institute was in trouble. A few people, privately, thought it had stopped moving.

Dr Meenal Joshi had been Director for eight months. Before that she had spent eighteen years at AITM, the last five as Dean of Academics, so she knew every corridor and every member of the leadership team. She had spent her first months mostly listening. One question she asked everyone was, "Why does your department exist?" Most people answered with a list of their activities. A few answered with a purpose. She noticed the difference and kept it to herself.

In the first week of November, the Secretary of the Society sent a one-page circular to the heads of its four institutes. Each was to submit a Graduate Outcome Report to the Society's Council by 15 December, in an attached format (Exhibit 1). The covering note said: "The Council wishes to understand what becomes of our graduates, so that we can learn from it. Please treat this as a priority." Dr Joshi forwarded it to her team with one line: "Let us discuss on Thursday."

Seven people met in the Director's office.

Rohan Pathak, Head of Training and Placement, spoke first, and he was relaxed. "Honestly, we already do this. Look at the format: placement percentage, median package, top recruiters, higher studies. I can fill every field by tomorrow." Rohan had built AITM's placement record over fifteen years: the employer relationships, three campus drives a week in season, and the mock interviews that students still described as the best thing the Institute did for them. The Chairman quoted the 87 per cent placement figure at every convocation.

Prof. Anil Deshpande, who had succeeded Dr Joshi as Dean of Academics, agreed. A mechanical engineer with twenty-six years of teaching, he had twice chaired the Board of Studies and was known for protecting the syllabus from passing fashions. "The Council has sent a format. Let us give them the format, on time. If anything in it concerns the academic side, it can come to the Board of Studies in the normal way."

Farida Shaikh, the Registrar, had already worked out the dates. If the data reached her by 1 December, the report would be signed and dispatched with a week to spare. Dr Kavita Rao, Director of Admissions and Marketing, asked only that nothing in the report contradict the brochure, since counselling for the next intake opened on 5 January. Vivek Naik, Controller of Finance, asked whether any new work or cost was involved. "If it is only a report, it is not really a finance matter," he said. Nobody thought it was more than a report.

Dr Joshi listened. This was a capable team, loyal to the Institute and respectful of one another. When she asked for something, it got done. She also had a feeling she could not quite put into words: that things got done exactly as asked, and no further. She noticed that nobody had asked what the Council meant by "learn from it".

At the end of the table, taking the minutes, sat Nandini Gokhale, an assistant manager in Alumni Relations. She had joined AITM eighteen months earlier from a bank, and she was at the meeting only because the Director liked a record kept. She said nothing.

"So we are fine," said Rohan, closing his diary.

"Draft by 1 December," said Dr Joshi.

## Phase B: Forty phone calls

Nandini's task, from the Registrar's office, was to supply "alumni contact details for the outcome report". The placement cell's list for last year had 612 graduates marked "placed". Coming from a bank, where a list always had a purpose, Nandini found it hard to send names without knowing what they were for. She also had a personal reason. Her cousin had graduated from AITM two years earlier, had been placed in a campus drive in her final year, and was now in another city in a job that had nothing to do with her degree. "In the report she is a success," Nandini thought. "She does not think so."

So, in the evenings and over a few lunch breaks, Nandini began to call. Over ten days she rang forty graduates from the placed list, chosen across every programme, and reached thirty-one. She asked each the same three questions. Are you still in the job you were placed in? What from AITM has been most useful? What do you wish had been different? One graduate told her to "ask the companies, not us", so she also called two employers who recruited at AITM every year. She told nobody, because nobody had asked her to.

Her notes (Exhibit 2) were not what anyone expected. Thirteen of the thirty-one were still with their first employer. Twelve had moved, eight of them within six months. Six were not working in their field at all. The comments were mixed. Several graduates praised the mock interviews and named people in the placement team. Others said things that did not appear in any report: "The campus drive was about getting an offer. Nobody asked me what I wanted to do." One employer said, "Good fundamentals. They need three months before they can write a client email or run a meeting." The other said, "We keep coming back because Rohan's team is so reliable. If the students were more rounded, we would hire more."

Nandini wrote a one-page note. Unsure whom to send it to, she emailed it to Rohan, "because it is about placed students". She copied no one.

Rohan read it on the bus home and was irritated before he had finished. The next morning he took it to the Dean's office, and Anil read it standing up. The two had worked together for nine years and were always cordial. Last year, to suit a recruiter's dates, Rohan had moved a final-year aptitude test into the week of Anil's internal examinations without telling him. Anil had said nothing at the time. Since then he had been a little slower to answer Rohan's emails.

"Thirty-one people," Rohan said. "That is a feeling, not a finding. Placement means offers. What a graduate does in his second year at a company is not in my hands. No institute reports that."

"Quite right," said Anil. "And if anybody wants to suggest that the syllabus is the problem, I would want a proper study through the Board of Studies, not a telephone survey from Alumni Relations."

"Then we agree," said Rohan.

"We do," said Anil. They did not discuss what, if anything, should happen next, or whose job it would be. Later that week, at a function, each told a colleague privately that the other's area was really where the problem lay.

The note travelled. Kavita heard of it and said quietly to the Director, "If this goes into a Council document, it will be on a parents' WhatsApp group by New Year." Vivek pointed out that real graduate tracking would need two people full time, which this year's budget did not have. Farida observed that the Council's format had no field for any of this, and that the report as drafted was accurate. All three were right.

Dr Joshi saw the note on Friday, when the Registrar's office sent it up "for information". She read it twice. She had no way of knowing whether thirty-one graduates were typical. She did know that she had read AITM's placement report every year for twelve years, the last five as Dean, and that it had never contained a sentence like "nobody asked me what I wanted to do".

## Phase C: The Monday meeting

By the following week the draft was ready. Rohan had filled every field of the Council's format in an afternoon. Everything in it was accurate. It would be submitted on time, and it would pass.

That weekend, two messages reached the Director. The Secretary of the Society wrote: "I only need the format, filled in on time. Please see that we look good." On Sunday evening, after a function, the Chairman stopped her and said something different: "I want our institutes to lead, not merely comply." He had not seen the replies to the circular and did not say what he meant.

On Monday morning Dr Joshi called Nandini in. Nandini was nervous. "Did I cross a line?"

"No," said the Director. "But I do not yet know what you have started."

The leadership team would meet at eleven. Dr Joshi wanted to leave the meeting with a decision, not, as she put it, "a discussion about whether to discuss". The draft was due to the Registrar by 1 December, the report to the Council by 15 December, and admissions counselling opened on 5 January. In her notebook she had written four options.

::: {custom-style="Commit Box"}
**Predict before you read the options.** What will this team most probably decide on Monday, and why? Write one line.
:::

**Option A. Submit the report as drafted, and record the finding.** AITM submits the accurate report in the Council's format, on time. Nandini's note is recorded in the minutes, and the Director asks Rohan and Anil to consider, after the admissions season, whether a follow-up study is needed. *What it offers:* the Institute meets its commitment cleanly, the admissions season is protected, and no department is put on trial on the strength of thirty-one phone calls. *What it costs:* the finding rests in the minutes. Next year's graduates go through the same process, and the people who noticed something learn what happens to inconvenient news.

**Option B. Submit on time, and run a 90-day pilot in one programme.** AITM submits the report as drafted, with a one-page note saying that the Institute will test a graduate follow-up method in its MBA programme and report back to the Council in March. A small team of Rohan, a faculty member nominated by Anil, and Nandini agrees three measures before it starts. *What it offers:* learning at small cost and small risk, evidence instead of anecdote, and no damage to the admissions message. *What it costs:* busy people must find time in their busiest season. It is not clear who leads a team that crosses three departments, and a pilot that finds problems will have to be acted on.

**Option C. Redesign "outcomes" with the people closest to them.** AITM submits an interim report on 15 December and tells the Council that a fuller one will follow in March. Before that, the leadership team visits four recruiters together, and in mid-December the Institute holds a two-day working session with twelve faculty members, ten recent graduates and eight employers. The aim is to agree what AITM should know about its graduates, and who in the Institute owns each part. *What it offers:* ownership well beyond the leadership team, and the largest possible improvement. *What it costs:* it is slow and visible. Weaknesses will be discussed in front of outsiders, the Secretary may be displeased, and expectations may rise beyond what the budget can meet.

**Option D. Ask the Society to decide.** AITM sends the draft report to the Secretary together with Nandini's note, and asks the Council whether it wants a fuller study, who should lead it, and whether outside help should be brought in. *What it offers:* the authority and neutrality of the Council, and a decision that does not rest on any one department. The question may, after all, apply to all four institutes. *What it costs:* the decision, and with it the ownership, moves out of the Institute. It is slower, and it may be read as a team that could not decide.

# Teaching note (for the facilitator only)

## Purpose

This is the first session of a fortnightly series for senior leaders. It aims to bring two behaviours to the surface without naming or judging them: carrying out a task without asking what it is for ("we already do this"), and cordial relations that stop short of shared ownership ("that is not my department"). The larger aim is for participants to see themselves as institutional leaders, not only as heads of departments. One session will not change habits. It should open questions the series can build on.

## Before the session

- **Group.** 12 to 24 people, seated in groups of four to six. For more than 24, add table reporters.
- **Print.** Phase A, Phase B, Phase C with options, and the exhibits as separate handouts. Give each person the participant pack at the start, with the Mirror sheet on top.
- **Role cards** for Phase B: Dr Joshi, Rohan, Anil, Kavita, Vivek, Farida, Nandini. Give each person a role far from their own job. For example, a finance head plays Nandini and a placement head plays Anil.
- **Ground rules, said aloud.** The case is fictional. No one's institute, department or performance is being reviewed. Argue positions, not people. Seniority does not decide the answer. Nobody is asked which character they resemble.

## Phase A: questions and probes

1. *What did the Council ask for, and what did it say it wanted?* The format asks for numbers; the covering note asks to "learn". Put both on the board.
2. *Rohan says "we already do this". Is he right?* Expect most of the room to agree, and they are partly right: placement data is real and hard-won. Probe: "What does the report measure, and what does it not measure?" "How would we know if the outcome were not good?"
3. *What did Dr Joshi notice, and what did nobody ask?* Draw out "exactly as asked, and no further".

**If you hear "we already do this" about real practice:** agree with what is true, then ask "What evidence would show it is working? What would a newcomer question?" Never correct it as wrong. The point is curiosity, not blame.

## Phase B: questions and probes

1. *In role: what is your character worried about, and what is your character right about?* Every character is right about something. Make sure each gets said.
2. *Rohan and Anil "agree". What did they agree on, and what did they avoid?* They agreed the finding was weak. They avoided the question of what to do and whose job it was. The aptitude-test story shows the cost of a disagreement that was never voiced.
3. *Whose problem is this?* Probe: "Is 'not my department' true? Is it useful? What would helping beyond your role look like for each character?"
4. If someone says Nandini overstepped, ask: "What do we want her to do next time? What will she actually do next time?"

## Phase C: options

Take the prediction poll first; most rooms predict Option A. Then poll the options. Ask one advocate per option for the strongest case, then ask the same person for the strongest case against it.

- **A.** On time, accurate and safe. *What does it teach Nandini?* A is defensible if the follow-up is real and has a date.
- **B.** *Who leads? What would make the pilot fail? How would we treat a pilot that brings bad news?*
- **C.** *What does inviting outsiders change? What is the risk, and who carries it?* This echoes Microsoft's leadership team visiting customers together.
- **D.** *When is escalation the right move, and when is it a way of not owning?* D has a real case: the question applies to all four institutes.

There is no right answer. The learning is in what each person needed to believe to choose their option.

## Mirror reveal and debrief

Ask everyone to open the sealed sheet and complete section 4 of the pack. Share one gap of your own first. Invite two or three volunteers; do not call on anyone. Typical gaps: rating S2 at 5 and choosing A; rating S3 at 5 while, in role, agreeing with Rohan in the corridor. Chris Argyris called this the difference between the theory we espouse and the theory we use.

Now name the idea for the first time: the difference between a mindset that protects what we already know and one that treats every task as a chance to learn (Carol Dweck's "fixed" and "growth" mindsets). Use the case to show the **advantages** of the second:

| Advantage | Where it showed in the case |
|---|---|
| Faster, cheaper learning | Forty calls in ten days surfaced what years of reports had not |
| Ownership beyond the brief | Nandini acted on the purpose, not the instruction |
| Using the people closest to the problem | Graduates, recruiters and a newcomer held the evidence |
| Fewer hidden problems | A report can be accurate and still miss the point: "it would pass" |
| Constructive disagreement | Rohan and Anil's polite agreement avoided the real question |
| Credibility with stakeholders | A recruiter said they would hire more: evidence can strengthen the brochure |
| Resilience when things change | A learning system adjusts each year; a format is just repeated |
| Keeping and growing talent | What Nandini learns now decides what the next Nandini does |

**Be honest about the limits.**

- Thirty-one calls do not prove a syllabus failure. Anil's demand for a proper study is legitimate, and rigour is part of learning.
- Not every task needs redesign. Sometimes the format is enough.
- A growth mindset is not positivity or praising effort alone.
- It must not become a new label for criticising colleagues. The Microsoft case describes Nadella stopping exactly this.
- On when to be firm: hold firm when the evidence is strong or ethics, safety or students' interests are at stake. Accommodate when a decision is low-cost and reversible. Help beyond your role when the institution's goal is at risk and your help is cheap compared with the cost to others.

## Bridge: Nadella and Argyris (10 minutes)

Use your own words. The facts below are from the London Business School case (Ibarra, Rattan and Johnston, 2018). Quote no more than shown.

- Satya Nadella became Microsoft's CEO in February 2014. He was an insider of 22 years, like Dr Joshi. Microsoft was very profitable, but its own employees described a culture of internal fights and inertia.
- He spent much of his first year listening, and asked why the company existed.
- He drew on Carol Dweck's research and described the shift as moving from "know-it-alls" to "learn-it-alls".
- He did not impose a definition. Kathleen Hogan and 180 executives in 17 teams worked out what the idea would mean for Microsoft.
- He wanted the senior team to treat itself as each member's "first team, not just another meeting they attended". This is the clearest link to institutional leadership.
- He invited recently acquired founders to the executive retreat although their rank did not qualify them. They "asked questions" and were "ignorant of the history they were breaking". Nandini plays the same part here.
- When he made a public mistake, he told all employees he had "answered that question completely wrong".
- Leaders closed meetings by asking whether the meeting had been growth-minded or fixed-minded, and why.
- Even four years on, Nadella saw middle management as the missing link. Culture change is slow.

**Argyris (1991).** In "Teaching Smart People How to Learn", Argyris argued that highly capable professionals often fall back on defensive reasoning when learning is needed, because they have rarely had to learn from failure. Ask: "Where are we like Microsoft in 2014? What do we 'know' that may no longer be true?"

## Close and link to session 2

Use the meeting-close question from the Microsoft case: "Was this a learning discussion or a defending discussion? Why?" Each person writes two commitments (pack, section 5). Collect the anonymous "practice a newcomer would question" slips.

For session 2, each participant brings one current initiative from their own area. The questions will be: what is it for, and how would you improve it?

# Quality check and sources to verify

**Quality check**

1. Each phase ends on a decision or a commitment, and Phase C ends on four options.
2. No option is presented as best. Each has a stated advantage and a stated cost, and the "comfortable" Option A is defensible.
3. Every character is right about something: Rohan (placement data is real), Anil (thirty-one calls are not a study), Kavita (families read documents), Vivek (tracking costs money), Farida (the draft is accurate), Nandini (the report misses something), and the Secretary and Chairman (both want the Institute to do well).
4. The case text and participant pack never name the concept. It is named only in the debrief.
5. The institution, people and figures are invented, and nothing in the case was drawn from KK Wagh. If the Chairman will attend, decide whether to keep the Secretary and Chairman sending different signals in Phase C.
