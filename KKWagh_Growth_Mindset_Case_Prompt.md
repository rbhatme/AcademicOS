## THE PROMPT

**Role.** You are a case writer and leadership teacher with experience in Indian higher education. You write short, decision-forcing cases for senior leaders. You also write the facilitator's teaching note that goes with them.

**Purpose.** I am designing a series of fortnightly 90-minute sessions for the top management team of an education society that runs several institutions in Maharashtra. The aim of the series is to help these leaders think and act as institutional leaders, with curiosity, ownership and a willingness to improve what they already do. This is the first session. One workshop will not change behaviour. It should open useful questions we can build on.

**Audience.** [12 to 20] senior leaders: [for example directors, principals, deans, registrar, finance, HR, admissions, estate, IT]. They are capable, respectful and senior. Some may already feel they are "doing all that can be done". Treat them as experts, not as people who need fixing.

**The behaviours the case must bring to the surface** (without naming or judging them):

1. Willingness to carry out a task, but little curiosity about its purpose and little initiative to improve it. Typical language: "we are already doing this", "this is how it is done", "there is not much scope here".
2. Uncertainty about when to take a firm position, when to accommodate another view and when to help beyond one's own responsibility. Cordial relations that do not turn into shared ownership of results.

**Step 1: do this first, then stop.** Propose three different premises for the case. For each, give five lines: the institution (fictional), the initiative at its centre, who is in the room, the tension, and why it would work for this audience. Mix the two behaviours above. Recommend one and say why. Then wait for my choice. Do not write the case yet.

Premise seeds you may use or improve on: an assessment or outcomes redesign that every department says it already does; a falling-enrolment problem where a junior colleague proposes something unconventional; a cross-functional deliverable that two capable leaders see differently; a new technology or AI policy that everyone agrees with and nobody owns.

**Step 2: after I choose, write the full pack.**

### A. The case (4 to 5 pages, about 2,000 to 2,400 words plus one or two short exhibits)

- Fictional institution, fictional people. No real person, institution or regulator named. Mention accreditation or regulation only in general terms. Use plausible, varied Indian names.
- Build it in **three phases (A, B, C)**, optionally a short D. Each phase is about one page and ends with a "Pause and commit" box. Participants must write an answer before they receive the next phase.
  - **Phase A** sets the scene and the initiative. The team is willing and busy. Everyone has a reasonable view.
  - **Phase B** adds a complication that tests ownership and relationships: a result that is good enough on paper, a quiet signal that something is off, or two capable leaders who disagree about whose call it is.
  - **Phase C** forces a decision with a deadline.
- End with **3 or 4 distinct options**, each written in the same neutral voice and about 100 to 150 words. For each, give one real advantage and one real cost. Do not rank them. Do not label any as the "growth" option. At least one option must be the sensible, comfortable choice of continuing to do the current thing well. The range should run from "perfect and defend what we have", through "run a small pilot and learn", to "redesign with the people at the front line". Add a fourth that escalates or hands the decision to someone else.
- Every character must be right about something. No villain, no hero, no obviously correct answer. Leave real ambiguity and real stakes.
- Do not use the words "growth mindset", "fixed mindset" or "learn-it-all" anywhere in the case. The idea must be discovered in the debrief.
- Include at least three lines of ordinary dialogue that a real leader would recognise, such as "we already do that" or "that is not my department".

### B. Interactive devices (build them into the case and the sheets)

- A **Mirror sheet** before Phase A: three statements about what good institutional leadership looks like, rated privately and sealed.
- **Cold read** of each phase in silence on paper, then commit before discussion.
- **Prediction before reveal**: before Phase C, "what will the team probably do, and why?"
- A **role switch**: each participant argues the case from a character who is not like them.
- An **assumption audit** in the debrief: "what do we know here that may no longer be true?" and "what would a newcomer question?"
- At the end, the **Mirror reveal**: the sealed statements next to the option each person would actually choose. The gap between belief and action is the main learning.
- One anonymous poll point and one pair-share point. Both must work on paper and on a phone.

### C. Participant pack (2 pages)

The Mirror sheet, the commit boxes for each phase, an option scorecard (advantage, cost, what I would need to believe), and a closing reflection with two commitments to try before the next session.

### D. Facilitator's teaching note (3 pages)

- A 90-minute run of show with minutes, for example: Mirror sheet 5, Phase A and discussion 20, Phase B and discussion 20, Phase C and options 25, Mirror reveal and debrief 15, close and commitments 5.
- For each phase: the questions to ask, what you may hear (include "we are already doing this"), and probing follow-ups.
- How to handle defensiveness and silence. Rules for psychological safety: the case is fictional, the room is not being assessed, and seniority does not decide the answer.
- The debrief. Use the case to bring out the **advantages of a growth mindset** as observable consequences. Cover at least six, for example: faster learning from small failures, real ownership, better use of the people closest to the problem, fewer hidden problems, constructive disagreement, and resilience when plans change. Show where in the case each one became visible.
- Honest limits: a growth mindset is not blind positivity or praising effort alone, and sometimes a firm position is the right call. Tie this to the second behaviour above.
- A **10-minute bridge** after the case, in your own words, linking to Nadella's move from "know-it-all" to "learn-it-all" at Microsoft and to Argyris on why capable professionals become defensive when learning is needed. Use only the facts given in the attached note. Mark anything you cannot verify as "to verify". Do not invent statistics.
- A short link to the next fortnightly session.

### Style and quality rules

- British spelling. Plain English. No em dashes. No management jargon in the case text.
- Senior-leader tone: respectful, concise, no moralising, no caricature of any institution or person.
- Do not reproduce or closely paraphrase any published case (Nadella, HCL, Taj, Wipro, Aravind) or the Argyris article.
- Before you give me the final pack, run this check and report it in five lines: each phase ends on a decision; no option is obviously best; every character is right about something; the case text never names the concept; nothing could be read as describing a real person or institution.

**Output.** [Word document / PDF / Markdown]. Put the case, the participant pack and the teaching note in separate sections so I can print them separately.

## Why the prompt is built this way

- **Original case, not an adapted one.** The published cases are long and copyrighted. Their ideas are used, but the text is new.
- **Fictional but resonant.** A disguised institution lets people see themselves without feeling singled out.
- **Phased commitments.** This follows the "Mirror Workshop" design in your colleague's note: stated beliefs against likely actions.
- **No label on the growth option.** If the options are labelled, people choose the "right" one. We want them to choose honestly, then examine why.
- **Two steps.** The premise is the riskiest choice, so you approve it before the pack is written.
